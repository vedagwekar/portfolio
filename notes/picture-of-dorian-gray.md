people read *the picture of dorian gray* as a book about vanity. i don't think it is. dorian isn't punished for wanting to stay beautiful. he's punished for arranging a life in which nothing he does can touch him, and wilde's point is that a person nothing can touch has stopped being a person at all.

the bargain is simple. the portrait ages and dorian doesn't. every cruelty, every year, every bit of guilt goes onto the canvas, and his face stays the face basil painted. it sounds like freedom. it's actually the end of a self. what we call a self is mostly the record of what we've done, carried in the body and in the face and in the way other people look at us. dorian moves that record into a locked room upstairs. from then on he can act, but he can't become anything. he stays exactly the boy in the painting, which is another way of saying he stays nobody.

the book is full of people who would rather look than be looked at. lord henry never does anything. he talks, and dorian acts on it. wilde gives him the line that explains the whole novel:

> to influence a person is to give him one's own soul. he does not think his natural thoughts, or burn with his natural passions.

lord henry lends dorian a philosophy and keeps none of the consequences. dorian takes on the consequences and hides them in the attic. between the two of them, nobody ever has to stand in front of what they've done. that's the real horror. not that dorian is wicked, but that the whole arrangement is built so that wickedness never has to be seen by the one doing it.

## what can't be locked upstairs

i've been thinking about the places in my own life where the record can't be moved anywhere else.

when i started learning guitar, my fingertips hurt for weeks. then they hardened. those calluses are a portrait i can't hide. they're the exact shape of every hour i actually practised, and they don't care what i say about how much i practise. if i stop, they go away. the body keeps an honest account.

basketball is the same. you can tell people you work hard, but your legs in the fourth quarter know the truth. the game shows what the training was. there's no attic for that.

then there are places where the record can be hidden. a profile, a feed, a list of things i've built, a portfolio. none of these are lies, exactly. they're curated. they show the face and leave the canvas upstairs. the danger isn't that they're dishonest to other people. it's that they let me stop looking at the canvas myself. when i kept the youth policy council's website running, the parts nobody saw were the ones that actually mattered: the bugs i fixed late, the things i got wrong before i got them right. if i only remembered the finished site, i'd be learning from the face and not the painting.

## the knife

the ending is the part i keep coming back to. dorian finally decides to be free of the portrait, so he stabs it. the servants find a beautiful painting of a young man, and on the floor an old, ruined body they only recognise by its rings. he tried to kill the evidence and killed himself, because the evidence was him.

i think that's the most honest thing wilde says. you can't get rid of what you've done by refusing to look at it. you can only choose whether you look at it while there's still time to change, or whether it waits for you upstairs.

so the question the book leaves me with isn't "am i vain?" it's "where is my portrait, and when did i last go up and look at it?"
